[10] p4 x72: ## Target Industry: Canadian Logistics

[11] p4 x108: [[This project looks at logistics in Canada, mainly freight transportation and the digital services that firms use to book and coordinate shipments. Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (2018) defines logistics as the planning, implementation, and control of the movement and storage of goods, services, and related information, which in daily business links suppliers, plants, warehouses, carriers, and customers. Moving the freight is only part of the job.]] Distribution also breaks down when inventory records are wrong, when orders wait to be processed, or when the carrier and the customer are working from different information.

[12] p4 x108: The industry is closely tied to the domestic economy and to international trade, as transportation and warehousing contributed CAD 96.5 billion, or 4.3% of national GDP, in 2024 (Transport Canada, 2025b). In that same year Canada’s international merchandise trade reached approximately CAD 1.55 trillion, of which, measured by value, road transportation carried 46%, marine transport 21.1%, air 11.6%, and rail 10.5%, so the first set of figures describes the output of the transportation and warehousing sector, whereas the second describes the value of the merchandise flows that the network moves.

[13] p5 x108: Mordor Intelligence (2026) estimates that freight transportation accounted for 60.88% of the Canadian freight and logistics market in 2025, and that share, combined with the growing use of digital freight marketplaces, makes this field a reasonable starting point for examining employers and the qualifications they look for. Freightera, a Vancouver-based business-to- business platform on which shippers compare and book freight, offers a useful example of this type of service (Freightera, n.d.-a).

[23] p6 x72: ## 2021: Recovery Exposed Supply-chain Weaknesses

[24] p6 x108: Freight demand came back once the first COVID-19 restrictions eased, and Transport Canada (2022) recorded more port container traffic and more truck crossings than in 2020. [[The network stayed fragile, though. [25] p6 x72: ## 2022: Strong Market Value, Persistent Congestion

[26] p7 x108: [[In 2022 the market estimate rose to USD 105 billion, the largest annual gain of the period (Ken Research, n.d.). Disruption in world supply markets raised export demand for Canadian commodities, while imported goods kept terminals and inland connections under pressure]] (Transport Canada, 2023), and the average container journey from Shanghai to Toronto reached 39.9 days, the longest of the five years (Transport Canada, 2026). Freight rates and tight capacity explain part of the USD 8 billion increase, so the higher value does not mean that more goods moved or that service improved.

[27] p7 x72: ## 2023: Weaker Goods Demand and Labour Disruption

[28] p7 x108: Growth slowed to 2.9% in 2023 as inflation, higher financing costs, and softer spending reduced demand for some imported and industrial goods (Ken Research, n.d.; Transport Canada, 2024). The July labour dispute at British Columbia ports was especially disruptive, since merchandise imports cleared through those ports fell 18.5% relative to June and exports departing them fell 23.0%, according to figures that Statistics Canada (2023) did not seasonally adjust, and although transit from Shanghai to Toronto improved to 35.0 days, part of that relief reflected the reduced congestion that came as demand eased (Transport Canada, 2026).

[29] p7 x72: ## 2024: Recovery Differed by Corridor

[30] p7 x108: The market estimate reached USD 111 billion in 2024, an increase of 2.8% (Ken Research, n.d.), while freight volumes recovered in several transport modes with results that varied across Canadian gateways, as Transport Canada (2025a) reported higher container activity at major ports alongside continued interruptions from labour disputes and extreme weather. Online retail, for its part, remained a meaningful source of distribution demand, since Statistics Canada (2026a) measured CAD 73.7 billion in e-commerce operating revenue for 2024, a figure 9.0% above that of 2023.

[31] p7 x72: ## 2025: Trade Reorientation Reshaped Freight Flows

[32] p8 x108: Although the market estimate edged up just 0.9% in 2025, to USD 112 billion (Ken Research, n.d.), the geography of freight changed considerably, as Transport Canada (2026) found that Canadian merchandise exports to the United States fell about 5% while exports to other markets climbed roughly 16%. Truck entries into Canada decreased by about 5% and U.S.- linked rail freight weakened, but marine gateways gained from overseas trade, with the Port of Vancouver handling a record 170.4 million metric tonnes, 8% more than in 2024, and with container transit from Shanghai to Toronto improving to 33.3 days, although it remained noticeably slower than in 2019 (Transport Canada, 2026).

[33] p8 x72: ## Interpretation of the Historical Trend

[34] p8 x108: Over these five years, a rebound in demand first exceeded the practical capacity of parts of the network, after which shipping conditions normalized, freight activity weakened on some routes, and service reliability gradually improved, until by 2025 trade policy was changing the geography of freight demand, putting transborder trucking under pressure while ocean-oriented corridors gained business. [[With Shanghai–Toronto transit moving between 39.9 days in 2022 and 33.3 days in 2025, employers needed people who could track a container, warn the customer early, and find another route when a corridor slowed down.]]
